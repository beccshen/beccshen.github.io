Tending Sanctuary

Independent research project
Penny White Project Fund, Harvard Graduate School of Design

Culture and Animals Foundation Interview, Scholar in Residence for VINE Sanctuary

Internal GSD advisor: Francesca Benedetto
External project advisor: Pattrice Jones, co-founder of VINE Sanctuary

Tending Sanctuary is a multispecies ethnography of VINE Sanctuary, a place designed around an ethos that centers farmed animals as protagonists and co-creators. VINE is a 100-acre sanctuary in the re-forested mountains of Vermont, home to over 700 rescued, formerly farmed animals who use their newfound agency to form complex social and ecological relationships with humans, wild species, and the landscape itself. The book's chapters (The Sanctuary, The Landscape Architect, The Forest, The Soil, The Residents, and The Stewards) describe, illustrate, and map the landscape's physical attributes, together telling how residents self-actualize, build community, co-create spaces, and shape material and ecological cycles. Seeing the landscape as shaped by healing domesticated animals brings new awareness of what it means to practice a land ethic and an animal ethic together. This requires moving beyond a species lens, which treats each species as a monolith valued for its ecosystem contribution, toward a multispecies lens that recognizes more-than-human beings as socio-ecologically entangled while also holding inherent self-worth, histories, and worldviews. The work offers VINE as a model for designing sanctuary landscapes that foster individual and collective agency and the healing of both animals and land. It also contributes a methodological outcome, movement mapping, and an attitude of observation that recognizes nonhuman animals as sentient subjects with agency.

A note on Observation / Mapping
This project draws on immersed fieldwork, contributing to the growing genre of multispecies ethnography. To give observations a temporal dimension, I deployed a creative methodology which I call movement mapping, elaborated upon in The Residents chapter. After some time at VINE, I began tracing the movements and locations of selected residents at regular intervals (such as every 10 minutes) for about five contiguous hours per day. The maps' lines and marks capture time in which I was in direct relationship with, and fully attentive to, the residents. To the best of my ability, I followed careful research ethics guidelines described in a research workshop by VINE Sanctuary's affiliate scholars and read residents' body language.
